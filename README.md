# Yachtmaster Learning Project

## Purpose

Develop a long-term, AI-assisted learning programme based initially on Tom Cunliffe's *The Complete Yachtmaster*, with two complementary objectives:

1. Prepare progressively for an RYA Yachtmaster qualification, potentially Yachtmaster Coastal or Offshore.
2. Develop the understanding, practical judgement and decision-making skills needed for safe, competent real-world sailing.

The ultimate curriculum target is comprehensive coverage of the official RYA Yachtmaster syllabus for the qualification selected. That syllabus will define the required scope and provide the benchmark for assessing coverage and identifying gaps.

Tom Cunliffe’s *The Complete Yachtmaster* will provide the initial reading sequence and a principal learning resource. The programme will supplement it wherever necessary to cover syllabus requirements.

Conceptual understanding, realistic sailing scenarios and practical judgement will be the principal ways of learning and applying that material. The programme will track theoretical understanding separately from practical competence.

The programme should be enjoyable, sustainable and adapted to my particular learning characteristics. It should evolve through experimentation rather than being fully specified at the outset.

## 1. Personal learning characteristics

I am intellectually capable, a quick thinker, and able to absorb concepts, patterns and information that has meaningful relationships to other information.

However:

- I struggle with short-term working memory.
- I find apparently arbitrary facts difficult to retain.
- I learn much more effectively when information is connected to underlying principles, causes, consequences or other familiar concepts.
- I need to study in short, frequent bursts rather than prolonged sessions.

The learning system should exploit these strengths and accommodate these difficulties.

### Implications

- Prioritise conceptual understanding over rote memorisation.
- Explain why something is true, not merely what is true.
- Establish relationships between ideas.
- Connect unfamiliar facts to familiar principles.
- Introduce factual detail within a meaningful conceptual framework.
- Keep individual study sessions short.
- Use repetition, variation and progressively deeper exploration.
- Avoid unnecessary demands on working memory.
- Adapt the pace and difficulty to demonstrated understanding.

## 2. Learning philosophy

The central objective is not to memorise a textbook. It is to develop an integrated understanding of sailing and seamanship that can be applied reliably in real situations.

Learning should address three complementary dimensions.

### A. Conceptual understanding

Understanding how and why things work.

Examples:

- Why does a tidal stream alter a yacht's course over the ground?
- How do wind, tide and sea state interact?
- Why does a particular sail-handling technique work?
- What principles underlie a navigation calculation?

### B. Practical seamanship and judgement

Developing the ability to make appropriate decisions in realistic situations.

Examples:

- How would you approach an unfamiliar harbour in deteriorating conditions?
- What would you do if the weather changed unexpectedly?
- What information would you gather before making a decision?
- What factors would cause you to abandon or modify a passage plan?
- How would you prioritise several competing concerns?

This dimension should receive particular emphasis.

### C. RYA Yachtmaster competence

Developing the knowledge and techniques required for the relevant RYA qualification.

This includes:

- Navigation and pilotage
- Tides and tidal calculations
- Meteorology
- Collision regulations
- Passage planning
- Safety and emergency procedures
- Boat handling and seamanship
- Command decisions and crew management
- Other requirements of the relevant RYA syllabus

The programme should be progressively mapped from the outset to the official RYA Yachtmaster syllabus for the qualification selected, identifying areas of adequate coverage and areas requiring further work. Selecting Yachtmaster Coastal or Offshore will determine the precise syllabus used as the benchmark.

Knowledge and practical competence should be tracked separately. Answering questions correctly does not establish the ability to perform a manoeuvre aboard a yacht.

## 3. Initial source material

The principal starting text will be Tom Cunliffe's *The Complete Yachtmaster*.

I own a paper copy.

The intended process is to read the book progressively, chapter by chapter, using AI to help develop and assess understanding.

The AI may not have access to the complete text. Consequently:

- I can identify chapters and sections being studied.
- I can supply chapter headings, notes, extracts or photographs of pages when needed.
- AI can generate questions using its general knowledge of the subject.
- Where exact correspondence with Cunliffe's treatment matters, we should verify against the text.

The book provides the initial reading sequence; the official RYA syllabus defines the ultimate curriculum scope. Material beyond the book should be added wherever needed to cover syllabus requirements.

## 4. Question bank

Build a persistent, expanding bank of questions associated with chapters, topics and underlying concepts.

Initially, generate approximately 20 questions per chapter, then expand coverage over time.

Questions should cover several forms of understanding:

| Type | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Conceptual | Explain why something works |
| Relational | Understand connections between principles |
| Applied | Apply knowledge to a situation |
| Diagnostic | Recognise mistakes or flawed reasoning |
| Factual | Recall essential facts, rules or conventions |
| Procedural | Understand the sequence of actions |
| Scenario-based | Exercise practical judgement and decision-making |

Multiple-choice questions will provide the initial format because they are quick to answer, easy to administer and suitable for short sessions.

However, the system should not be restricted to multiple-choice questions indefinitely.

It should sometimes request a brief explanation of the reasoning behind an answer, particularly where the answer is incorrect, uncertain or reveals a potentially important misunderstanding.

### Initial question mix

A possible starting distribution for a 20-question chapter bank:

- 8 practical decision-making questions (40%)
- 5 conceptual and relational questions (25%)
- 4 RYA knowledge and technique questions (20%)
- 3 factual recall or procedural questions (15%)

These proportions are provisional and should vary with the subject.

### Question quality: require subject knowledge

The boat-handling-under-sail experiment exposed a weakness in the starter questions: many correct answers could be identified from common sense or sentence construction without understanding the sailing. The correct option often sounded organised, responsible and appropriately cautious, while the alternatives sounded reckless or made implausible absolute claims. A high score on such a set provides limited evidence of subject understanding.

Use this design test:

> Would someone who has studied this chapter, or acquired equivalent sailing knowledge, have a clear advantage over an intelligent reader relying only on common sense and clues in the wording?

The aim is to test learning from the chapter, without requiring recall of the author's wording or obscure details merely to make questions difficult. When only chapter headings are available, label questions as topic-based; do not claim that answering them demonstrates understanding of the chapter's actual treatment.

#### Design rules

- **Identify the knowledge being tested first.** For each question, be able to state the specific principle, relationship, technique or essential fact needed to answer it. If the deciding principle is only “be careful”, “prepare properly” or “brief the crew”, develop the question further.
- **Make the alternatives compete on sailing substance.** Use plausible errors in cause and effect, sail choice, sequence, geometry or interpretation. Each incorrect option should represent a recognisable misunderstanding, rather than an obviously foolish action. Do not invent misleading technical claims simply to fill four slots; use fewer options or a written question when necessary.
- **Remove wording clues.** Keep options comparable in length, detail, grammatical construction and tone. Do not make the correct answer the only qualified statement, the only comprehensive plan or the only one containing words such as “controlled”, “suitable” or “reassess”. Avoid using “always” and “never” mainly to make incorrect options easy to reject. Vary correct-answer positions without a predictable pattern.
- **Put shared good practice in the scenario.** If all competent approaches involve preparation, a crew briefing and an escape route, establish those as already done. Ask which specific action or interpretation fits the conditions. Good organisation matters, but selecting the only organised-sounding option does not demonstrate knowledge of how to organise this manoeuvre.
- **Make judgement depend on conditions.** Compare credible choices whose suitability turns on a stated detail. Ask what that detail changes, what observation would distinguish two explanations, or what would cause a different choice. More caution is not automatically the best answer; the action must address the actual situation, including any consequences of delay.
- **Use short written answers when recognition is too easy.** Ask for a prediction and its cause, a choice and its deciding reason, or the consequence of changing one condition. A sentence or two should normally suffice. Do not replace easy multiple choice with long essays or unnecessary working-memory demands.
- **Keep difficulty in the sailing.** State enough assumptions for one best answer, use plain language, and avoid trick wording, hidden conditions and obscure trivia. If two choices remain defensible, clarify the scenario or use an open question that accepts supported alternatives.

#### Review before adding a question

1. **Knowledge check:** What specific subject knowledge separates the intended answer from its strongest alternative?
2. **Common-sense check:** Could an intelligent non-sailor probably answer correctly without that knowledge? If so, revise it or retain it explicitly as an introductory check, rather than evidence of deeper understanding.
3. **Wording check:** Read the options without the scenario. Does one stand out as longer, more measured, more responsible or more complete? Remove that cue.
4. **Alternative check:** Explain why each incorrect option is tempting and exactly which stated condition or principle rules it out. Check that only one answer is best under the stated assumptions.
5. **Transfer check:** Across the set, include variations where a changed condition changes the answer, so that repeating a familiar slogan is insufficient.

For example, replace “Should you prepare, brief the crew and plan an escape, or continue unprepared?” with a question where those preparations are complete and the learner must predict how a specified change in wind angle affects the ability to depower a sail. Use similarly concise predictions for all options, or ask for a short explanation without providing options.

Some straightforward foundation checks remain useful, but a chapter set should predominantly require the chapter's subject knowledge. Do not treat an easy score as strong evidence of understanding when the questions themselves supplied shortcuts. Apply these rules to future sets and when existing sets are revised; the original starter banks have not yet been brought up to this standard.

The [question-shaping guide](question-shaping-guide.md) records further current preferences, including no confidence ratings and no persistent assessment outcomes.

## 5. Scenario-based learning

A particularly important component will be realistic, evolving sailing scenarios.

Instead of asking only what is technically correct, questions should ask:

- How would you approach this situation?
- What would you do first?
- What information do you need?
- What alternatives are available?
- What are the principal risks?
- What would cause you to change your decision?
- What would you do if the situation deteriorated?

Scenarios should sometimes develop through several stages.

For example:

1. A yacht approaches an unfamiliar harbour as conditions deteriorate.
2. The learner selects an approach and explains the reasoning.
3. Additional information becomes available.
4. A complication occurs.
5. The learner must reassess the situation and make another decision.

The aim is to develop anticipation, prioritisation, flexibility, risk awareness and sound command judgement.

A good answer should reflect good reasoning, not merely a fortunate choice.

Questions should acknowledge that real seamanship sometimes permits several defensible decisions, depending on circumstances.

## 6. Short daily study sessions

The eventual system should support short, frequent sessions, perhaps five to ten minutes per day.

A typical session might contain approximately eight questions, selected adaptively from the growing bank.

The selection should balance:

- Previously studied material due for review
- Concepts that have caused difficulty
- Recently introduced material
- Questions connecting material from different chapters
- Occasional unfamiliar variations on well-understood principles

The objective is to maintain knowledge over time without requiring long or burdensome study sessions.

Twenty questions may be appropriate for the initial chapter bank, but not necessarily for each daily session.

## 7. Adaptive learning and spaced repetition

The system should track performance over time and use it to influence future question selection.

It should distinguish between:

- Correct answers given confidently
- Correct answers reached through uncertainty or guessing
- Incorrect answers caused by missing factual knowledge
- Incorrect answers caused by conceptual misunderstanding
- Mistakes caused by misreading or working-memory demands

A simple confidence indication may be useful:

- Certain
- Reasonably confident
- Guessing

Questions and concepts should be revisited at appropriate intervals.

Repeated success should generally reduce review frequency. Difficulty or uncertainty should increase it.

However, repetition should not consist solely of presenting identical questions. Alternative formulations and scenarios should test whether the underlying understanding transfers to new situations.

## 8. Progressive chapter and syllabus coverage

The programme should distinguish between two separate measures:

**Coverage:** How comprehensively has the material in a chapter been sampled, and how much of the selected official RYA syllabus has been addressed? Chapter coverage and syllabus coverage should be tracked separately so that gaps outside the book remain visible.

**Mastery:** How reliably can the learner understand, recall and apply the material that has been sampled?

High scores on a small question bank do not necessarily demonstrate mastery of an entire chapter.

The intended process is therefore iterative:

1. Read a chapter or section.
2. Establish the important concepts and learning objectives, and map them to the relevant syllabus requirements.
3. Generate an initial question bank.
4. Complete short assessments.
5. Identify weaknesses and misunderstandings.
6. Refine or expand the questions.
7. Progress to additional chapters.
8. Periodically revisit earlier chapters to increase both coverage and mastery.
9. Review syllabus coverage and add material for requirements not adequately addressed by the chapters studied.

Over time, the questions should increasingly connect material from multiple chapters.

## 9. Feedback and explanation

Feedback should be constructive, concise and conceptually useful.

For incorrect answers, the AI should try to identify the underlying misunderstanding rather than simply state the correct answer.

Where appropriate, it should ask the learner to explain their reasoning.

Explanations should emphasise:

- Causal relationships
- Underlying principles
- Mental models
- Connections to previously understood material
- Practical implications

The AI should avoid lengthy explanations of concepts already understood.

It should also distinguish between situations where the learner needs a conceptual explanation and situations where a fact or procedure genuinely must be memorised.

## 10. Real-world sailing integration

The learning system should ultimately connect theory with actual sailing experience.

Potential features include:

- Pre-sail preparation questions
- Scenario exercises related to planned passages
- Post-sail reflection and debriefing
- Identification of practical skills needing attention
- Questions based on decisions encountered during sailing
- Tracking of practical experience separately from theoretical understanding

Where appropriate, exercises should reflect the capabilities and limitations of the yacht, crew and sailing conditions.

AI-generated advice must not be treated as a substitute for current charts, official navigational information, qualified instruction or judgement aboard the vessel.

## 11. Long-term automation

The eventual objective is a system that can prompt me, perhaps once a day on my laptop, to complete a short learning session.

It should:

- Maintain a persistent question bank.
- Record responses and confidence.
- Track chapters, concepts, official syllabus requirements, coverage and mastery.
- Select questions adaptively.
- Apply spaced repetition.
- Introduce cross-topic questions.
- Support progressive expansion of the material.
- Preserve continuity between sessions.

Two possible implementations are envisaged.

### Phase 1: ChatGPT-assisted prototype

Use ChatGPT to create and administer questions, with a persistent structured file containing the question bank and learning history.

A scheduled reminder could prompt daily study.

This phase would establish what works before substantial software development.

### Phase 2: Dedicated study application

If justified, develop a lightweight browser-based application providing:

- A persistent database
- Adaptive question selection
- Short interactive tests
- Progress and coverage tracking
- Scheduling and notifications
- AI-assisted question generation and explanations
- Import and export of learning data

The application should emerge from experience with the learning process, rather than dictate it prematurely.

## 12. Proposed first experiment

Begin with one chapter of *The Complete Yachtmaster*.

1. Read the chapter.
2. Provide the chapter title, edition and any necessary source material.
3. Ask AI to identify the principal concepts and create approximately 20 questions.
4. Complete several short assessment sessions.
5. Examine which question types are most useful.
6. Evaluate the balance between conceptual understanding, factual recall and practical judgement.
7. Refine the approach before formalising the question-bank structure and automation.

The first objective is to validate the learning method, not build software.

## 13. Matters to resolve

Several decisions remain open:

- Whether the eventual target is RYA Yachtmaster Coastal or Offshore.
- Which edition of *The Complete Yachtmaster* will be used.
- The most effective length of a daily session.
- Whether multiple-choice questions should initially dominate.
- How much reasoning should be requested after answers.
- How best to distinguish conceptual difficulty from memory difficulty.
- How to record confidence without adding friction.
- How to measure chapter coverage and official syllabus coverage.
- How to connect the learning programme to actual sailing.
- What form of reminders and persistent storage will work best.

These should be resolved progressively through experimentation.

## 14. Guiding principle

**The aim is to become a better skipper, not merely a better examination candidate.**

The programme should build a connected body of knowledge, develop reliable practical judgement, and progressively establish the knowledge and skills expected of an RYA Yachtmaster.

It should favour understanding over memorisation, realistic decisions over artificial exercises, and sustained learning over intensive short-term revision.

Above all, it should adapt to the learner, remain manageable in short sessions, and become more useful as experience accumulates.
