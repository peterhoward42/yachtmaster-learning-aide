# Boat handling under power — starter questions

Topic-based questions organised by the recorded headings for chapter 6 of Tom Cunliffe’s *The Complete Yachtmaster*, **10th edition (2021), printed pages 44–59**.

These 24 questions follow the [coverage model](../cunliffe-coverage-model.md): 22 across eleven subsections, plus two integration questions. The full chapter text was not available, so correspondence with Cunliffe’s specific explanations remains unverified. This is not a comprehensive assessment of the official RYA syllabus.

## Instructions

- Assume a displacement cruising monohull with a single fixed shaft-driven propeller **ahead of a separate rudder**, unless stated otherwise. No sails are set. Do not assume the same handling for a saildrive, steerable outboard or twin-engine boat.
- Port and starboard refer to the yacht, including when she moves astern. A wheel turned to starboard means the rudder setting that turns the bow to starboard when moving ahead.
- Each multiple-choice question has **three options and one best answer**. Questions 2, 6, 8, 10, 14, 18, 20 and 24 require a short written answer.
- Assume lines and fenders are prepared, the crew understand their tasks, and there is an escape plan. Choose between the handling effects described, rather than adding generic preparation advice.
- Where a scenario specifies observed behaviour, use that observation rather than assuming another yacht behaves identically.
- Try four short sessions: **1–6, 7–12, 13–18, then 19–24**. The answer key is omitted for the initial attempt.

## Pivoting — C06.S01

### 1. Which end needs clearance?

Moving slowly ahead parallel to a pontoon on your port side, you turn the bow to starboard. Before the yacht has moved far, which movement must you allow for at the other end?

A. The stern initially swings to starboard, following the bow away from the pontoon.

B. The stern initially swings to port, towards the pontoon as the yacht turns.

C. The stern initially stays on the old track while only the bow changes direction.

**My answer (A–C):** B

## Blowing off — C06.S02

### 2. What happens while you wait?

Trials show that, when stopped beam-on to the wind, this yacht’s bow blows downwind faster than her stern. You stop with the wind coming from port and leave the engine in neutral.

Which way will the bow turn initially? Why could a pause in this attitude make a subsequent attempt to motor directly into the wind harder?

**My answer (one or two sentences):** Bow will move to starboard. That turn is pointing her further downwind and requires a greater subsequent turn in order to point into the wind.

## Rudder effect — C06.S03

### 3. Turning before gathering much way

The yacht is nearly stationary in calm water. You set the rudder to turn the bow to starboard and give a brief burst ahead. A turn begins before appreciable forward speed builds. What principally gives the rudder its initial effect?

A. The propeller stream flows aft over the angled rudder.

B. The rudder shifts the propeller thrust bodily to starboard.

C. The keel redirects the propeller stream onto the bow.

**My answer (A–C):** A

### 4. The engine is astern; the yacht is not

You are still moving ahead through the water after selecting astern to brake. Consider only the rudder force due to that continuing forward motion, excluding propeller effects. With the wheel held to starboard, which way does that force tend to turn the bow?

A. To port, because astern gear reverses the steering effect.

B. Neither way, because ahead motion and astern gear cancel it.

C. To starboard, because the yacht still has forward water flow.

**My answer (A–C):** C

## Propeller effects — C06.S04

### 5. Which berth suits the kick?

Trials establish that a burst astern kicks this yacht’s stern to port. Two long, empty pontoons offer otherwise identical approaches in calm water. You will arrive moving ahead and use astern to stop. At which berth would that kick tend to bring the stern **towards** the pontoon?

A. Starboard side to the pontoon.

B. Port side to the pontoon.

C. Both sides equally once the yacht slows.

**My answer (A–C):** B

### 6. Separate two causes

In calm water you suspect that a yacht’s initial swing when reversing is mainly prop walk, rather than rudder steering. Describe a simple comparison you could make in open water to distinguish these effects. Include the movement you would look for.

**My answer (two or three sentences):** With the boat stationary apply a short burst of reverse with the helm to port. Repeat the same test with the helm to starboard. If the initial swing is mainly from prop walk the stern will swing in the same direction in both tests. 

## Moving ahead — C06.S05

### 7. Burst, then neutral

In calm water, two approaches produce the same low forward speed. One uses brief ahead bursts separated by neutral; the other stays in ahead continuously. During a neutral interval, what supplies the rudder’s steering effect?

A. Water flow from the yacht’s continuing forward motion.

B. Residual propeller wash maintained throughout neutral.

C. Propeller side force redirected by the stationary shaft.

**My answer (A–C):** A

## The set piece short turn — C06.S06

### 8. Choose the direction

In clear water, trials show strong stern-to-port prop walk in astern and a useful starboard bow turn from an ahead burst with starboard rudder. You want a tight 180° turn with little net forward travel.

Which direction of turn lets these two effects assist one another? Explain how the ahead and astern phases contribute. Exact throttle settings are not required.

**My answer (two or three sentences):** Active movement of the stern in reverse is only available to port. That induces a boatwise clockwise turn. and the clockwise turn can be augmented with short ahead bursts with starboard rudder. The switching between forward and reverses tends to minimise forward travel.

## Motoring astern — C06.S07

### 9. Steering after the kick

You have established useful sternway, but the propeller’s side force is pulling the stern off the intended track. There is room to continue coasting astern. What is the immediate effect of selecting neutral?

A. It reduces both prop walk and sternway to zero together.

B. It removes rudder flow while preserving propeller side force.

C. It removes powered prop walk while sternway still supplies rudder flow.

**My answer (A–C):** C

### 10. Reverse the water flow

Ignore wind, tide and prop walk. The yacht is moving steadily astern with the rudder amidships. You turn the wheel to starboard.

Which way will the **stern** initially move, and which way will the **bow** swing? Explain using the reversed flow over the rudder.

**My answer (one or two sentences):** The aft edge of the rudder is "pointing" towards starboard. If the water flow over the rudder from the boat's way was in the normal forward direction this would move the stern to port and thus cause the boat to pivot about roughly its centre - pointing the bow more to starboard. But in this steady astern boat movement that effect is reversed.

## Berthing — C06.S08

### 11. Slow over the ground

A steady two-knot stream flows along a pontoon. You can approach either heading directly into the stream or directly with it. In each case your closing speed along the pontoon is half a knot over the ground. Which approach provides forward water flow over the rudder?

A. Heading into the stream; the yacht moves ahead through the water.

B. Heading with the stream; the stream adds to forward rudder flow.

C. Both headings; equal ground speeds give equal forward rudder flow.

**My answer (A–C):** Consider the into the stream case. For the closing speed to be half a knot, you must be motoring through the water at 2.5 knots. I.e. 2 knots to stem the stream, plus an additional 0.5 know to close. In the with-it scenario you would need 2 knots of reverse to stem the stream minus 0.5 knot to close. To the former provides forward flow over the rudder. (This is a very odd question).

### 12. Crossing the stream

You are holding position over the ground while pointing directly into a uniform stream. You then angle the bow slightly towards a pontoon on your starboard side and adjust power to prevent movement along the pontoon. Ignore wind. What happens?

A. The yacht moves to port because the stream meets her port bow.

B. The yacht moves to starboard while making no progress along the pontoon.

C. The yacht remains in the same position because power cancels the stream.

**My answer (A–C):** B (i.e. ferry gliding)

### 13. A crosswind changes the approach

You are approaching an empty berth with space to its windward side. The wind blows off the pontoon, carrying the yacht away from it. Compare this with the same manoeuvre in calm water. Before a line is attached, which adjustment addresses the sideways drift?

A. Aim farther downwind of the intended track and allow the wind to turn the bow back.

B. Keep the calm-water heading and use an earlier burst astern to stop the sideways drift.

C. Aim to windward of the intended track by an amount that offsets the observed drift.

**My answer (A–C):** C

### 14. Can this gap take the turn?

A berth is longer than the yacht, but only just. You can put the bow into the gap at an angle; bringing the whole yacht parallel would require the stern to swing past the boat immediately behind it.

Why is berth length alone insufficient to establish that this approach will work? Name the extra clearance you need to assess.

**My answer (one or two sentences):** Not sure about this one. But our boat must be clear fwd of the astern boat to complete the swing and still carrying forward motion to provide the steerage required. To the available length must also allow the distance required to slow down and stop.

## Coming alongside — C06.S09

### 15. The last burst astern

You approach **starboard side to** a pontoon in calm water. Your yacht kicks her stern to port when astern power is applied. Just before braking, which stern position gives room for that expected movement while finishing approximately parallel?

A. The stern slightly farther from the pontoon than its final position.

B. The stern slightly nearer the pontoon than its final position.

C. The stern at its final position with the bow angled away from the pontoon.

**My answer (A–C):** B. But how can you get into this position? Are you assuming you can approach parallel to the pontoon?

### 16. Which way should the first line lead?

You are alongside with slight residual forward movement. A line from a strong point near the bow is to check that movement. In which direction should its pontoon attachment lie relative to the onboard attachment?

A. Aft, so the loaded line has an aft component of pull on the yacht.

B. Ahead, so the loaded line has a forward component of pull on the yacht.

C. Directly abeam, so the loaded line has a transverse component of pull.

**My answer (A–C):** A

### 17. A line under ahead power

You are port side to a pontoon. A short line leads from a strong point near the bow **aft** to a pontoon cleat. The bow is suitably fendered. Other lines are slack. You apply gentle ahead power with the rudder set to turn the bow to port. Which tendency is produced by the propeller wash acting on the rudder?

A. The stern is pushed to port, bringing it closer to the pontoon.

B. The bow is pushed to starboard, taking it away from the pontoon.

C. The stern is pushed to starboard, taking it away from the pontoon.

**My answer (A–C):** C. But it would do this with that spring arrangement even with a neutral rudder

### 18. Wind on or wind off?

Compare two otherwise identical arrivals alongside an empty pontoon in negligible tide. In one, the wind presses the yacht onto the pontoon; in the other, it blows her away from it. Both arrivals finish with the yacht stopped, parallel and within easy line-handling distance.

In which case is prompt attachment of a suitable line more critical to keeping the yacht within reach? Explain the difference in what happens while the crew handle the line.

**My answer (one or two sentences):** Prompt line handling is required for the blown-off scenario to prevent the boat drifting too far from the pontoon for the crew to get onto it. In this case the lines will be loaded,
so the crew need to cleat or snub them promptly to avoid carrying the load manually.

## Leaving an alongside berth — C06.S10

### 19. Springing the stern out

You are starboard side to a pontoon with little space ahead or astern. A line runs from a strong point near the bow **aft** to a pontoon cleat. The bow is fendered for the manoeuvre. You want to swing the stern out before reversing clear. Which combination uses ahead propeller wash to assist that swing while the line checks forward travel?

A. Ahead power with the rudder set to turn the bow to port.

B. Ahead power with the rudder set to turn the bow to starboard.

C. Astern power with the rudder set to turn the bow to starboard.

**My answer (A–C):** B

### 20. A different departure

You are port side to a pontoon in calm water, with adequate room ahead. A line runs from a strong point near the stern **forward** to a pontoon cleat, and the stern is fendered. Gentle astern power tensions the line and trials show that this arrangement swings the bow away from the pontoon.

Once the bow has enough clearance, what change of propulsion would take the yacht out bow first? At what stage should the remaining line be released and recovered relative to applying departure power?

**My answer (two short points):** Switch to power fwd. The spring should be retrieved just beforehand to avert the risk of it fouling the prop.

## Bow thrusters — C06.S11

### 21. Turning or sliding?

A nearly stationary yacht in calm water has a bow thruster but no stern thruster. With engine neutral and rudder amidships, the thruster pushes the bow to starboard. Which initial motion best describes its effect?

A. Sideways translation to starboard without a change in heading.

B. A turn to port combined with sideways movement to starboard.

C. A turn to starboard combined with sideways movement to starboard.

**My answer (A–C):** C

### 22. Which end can it reach?

You are stopped almost parallel to a pontoon on your starboard side. The bow is already at the desired distance, but the stern is too far out. Neglect wind and stream. What is the initial rotational effect of using the bow thruster to push the bow towards the pontoon?

A. The stern tends to swing farther away from the pontoon.

B. The stern tends to swing towards the pontoon with the bow.

C. The stern tends to stay fixed while the bow moves sideways.

**My answer (A–C):** A

## Integration

### 23. Change the propeller effect

Two otherwise similar yachts have equally effective ahead propeller wash over their rudders. Yacht P kicks her stern to port in astern; yacht S kicks hers to starboard. Each is to make a short turn using alternating ahead and astern phases. Which pairing lets the astern kick assist the chosen bow turn?

A. P turns to port; S turns to starboard.

B. P turns to starboard; S turns to port.

C. Both turn to starboard using different rudder angles astern.

**My answer (A–C):** B

### 24. The wind changes the diagnosis

You practise reversing in open water. In calm conditions, the initial stern-to-port kick becomes manageable once sternway builds and you select neutral. In a crosswind, the bow continues blowing downwind during that neutral coast, despite a similar speed through the water.

What additional effect now needs to be accounted for? Why would “select neutral to remove prop walk” be an incomplete solution this time? Name one change you could test in open water to improve control.

**My answer (two or three sentences):** I'm stumped by that one.

## Source and design notes

Section allocation comes from the repository’s [coverage model](../cunliffe-coverage-model.md) and [contents reference](../cunliffe-contents-reference.md). General principles of rudder flow, backing and propeller side force were cross-checked against the US Coast Guard’s [Boat Crew Handbook — Seamanship Fundamentals, chapter 6](https://rdept.cgaux.org/documents/BoatCrewHandbooks/BoatCrewBCH16114.4.pdf). This supplementary reference covers other vessel types as well; the questions state yacht-specific assumptions and observed behaviour where needed. It does not verify Cunliffe’s treatment.

The set applies the README’s question-quality rules: three alternatives where useful, matched wording, specified line geometry instead of ambiguous spring names, and short written explanations where options would give away the reasoning. Related questions change direction, flow or prop-walk behaviour to test transfer. They assess theoretical reasoning, not demonstrated competence aboard a yacht.
