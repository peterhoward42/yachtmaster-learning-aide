# Sailing in heavy weather — starter questions

Topic-based questions organised by the recorded headings for chapter 10 of Tom Cunliffe’s *The Complete Yachtmaster*, **10th edition (2021), printed pages 95–102**.

These 12 questions follow the [coverage model](../cunliffe-coverage-model.md): one opening question, two each on preparing the boat, sails and steering in waves, three on survival tactics, and two integration questions. The full chapter text was not available, so correspondence with Cunliffe’s specific explanations remains unverified. This is an initial topic sample, not a comprehensive assessment of the chapter or official RYA syllabus.

## Instructions

- Assume a seaworthy ballasted cruising monohull sloop, unless stated otherwise. Equipment and manoeuvres must suit the particular yacht.
- Multiple-choice questions have three options and one best answer. Questions 3, 5, 7, 9 and 12 need a short written answer.
- Crew preparation, lifejackets, harness arrangements and a lookout are already in place. Concentrate on the particular sailing problem in each question.
- Try two short sessions: **1–6, then 7–12**. The answer key is omitted for the initial attempt. No confidence ratings are needed.

## Opening material — C10.S01

### 1. The wind has eased

After a front passes, the wind drops substantially and changes direction. Large waves from the earlier wind remain, with a new wave pattern beginning to develop. Which observation should govern whether to increase sail now?

A. The lower wind speed: sail area can return to its earlier setting before the front.

B. The yacht’s steering and motion in the remaining seas: lighter wind may coexist with difficult waves.

C. The new wind direction: the remaining waves will turn to align with it as the yacht changes course.

**My answer (A–C):** B

## Preparing the boat — C10.S02

### 2. A lid is not a fastening

A cockpit locker communicates with the yacht’s interior. Its lid is shut but its latch does not engage. Why is this a flooding concern during a knockdown even if the cockpit drains are clear?

A. The locker creates an airlock that prevents water reaching the cockpit drains.

B. The locker makes the cockpit drains act as seawater inlets whenever the yacht heels.

C. The lid can open under immersion, giving water a route below that bypasses the drains.

**My answer (A–C):** C

### 3. Secured for upright sailing

A heavy toolbox sits low down, wedged firmly between furniture, but has no restraint above it. It does not slide when the yacht heels normally.

Why does that fail to establish that it is secured for a knockdown? Describe the direction of movement its restraint must also prevent, and one consequence of overlooking it.

**My answer (two or three sentences):** The direction of upwards when the yacht is level becomes near horizontal when the yacht heels to the degree it does during a knockdown. So to prevent the toolbox from sliding in that case it must be secured in a way that prevents its upward movement if the yacht were upright. If the heavy toolbox were able to slide around during a knockdown it presents a damage and safety hazard to people and objects in the cabin.

## Sails — C10.S03

### 4. The small sail that still pulls badly

A partly furled genoa has become deeply baggy. On a close reach it produces substantial heel but poor drive. The yacht also has a purpose-made heavy-weather jib, with a suitable stay and sheet leads, which the crew can still set. What is the strongest sailing reason for changing to it?

A. Its cut and construction can preserve a useful shape at the required small sail area.

B. Its smaller area will create the same drive regardless of its sheeting angle.

C. Its separate stay will remove the mainsail’s contribution to weather helm.

**My answer (A–C):** A

### 5. Same wind, different course

A yacht is running directly downwind at 7 knots in a steady 30-knot true wind. The skipper proposes turning onto a close reach without first reviewing the sail area. Ignore gusts and wave-induced speed changes for this question.

How will the apparent wind experienced by the yacht change as it turns, and why does the comfortable downwind sail plan give an unreliable guide to the sail area needed after the turn? No calculation is required.

**My answer (two or three sentences):** Apparent wind is the wind strength and direction "felt" by the boat, comprising two components. One the true wind, the other the reciprocal of the boat's own movement. When sailing downwind the boat-movement is in the same direction of the wind, thus reducing the apparent wind's strength. When turning upwind however the apparent wind direction changes to be be more abeam and then forward and increases in strength. The changing and new regime may overpower the old sail plan.

## Steering in waves — C10.S04

### 6. Equal wind angles, unequal waves

Two possible broad-reaching courses offer equal apparent wind angles and equally clear water. On one, the dominant breaking seas approach nearly abeam; on the other, they approach from the stern quarter. Which is the stronger reason to favour the second course, assuming the yacht remains steerable at that speed?

A. A stern-quarter sea eliminates the possibility of an accidental gybe.

B. A beam-on breaking sea presents a greater risk of rolling the yacht into a knockdown.

C. Equal apparent wind angles give equal wave loading, so only the distance sailed matters.

**My answer (A–C):** B

### 7. A compass course, but diminishing control

Running in large following seas, the yacht accelerates down wave faces. The helmsman needs progressively larger corrections, and on several waves the yacht yaws far enough to expose its beam to the approaching crest. The average compass course still matches the passage plan.

What do these observations tell you that the average course does not? Name one change to sail area or heavy-weather tactic you would consider, explaining the control problem it is intended to address.

**My answer (two or three sentences):** I haven't learned about this per se, but I will attempt an answer based on stuff I do know. Please educate me when I later ask for your assessment. This situation you describe is perilous because of the risk of broaching and dealing with that is more important than than adhering to the planned passage plan. The safest course remains downwind, so the challenge is more about how to maintain that boat pointing direction and to moderate its speed. Changing the sail plan to jib or storm jib alone will reduce yawing and maintain downwind pointing better. That sail area reduction will reduce speed but only to a point because part of the speed is coming from the boat surfacing on the waves. In extremis sailing under "bare poles" alone might provide sufficient forward drive and a slower speed. An additional contribution to both directional stability and reducing speed would be to tow a drogue (improvised or propriatary) astern.

## Survival tactics — C10.S05 

### 8. What balances the backed jib?

In trials on this yacht, a backed small jib tends to push the bow away from the wind. The yacht retains enough forward movement for the rudder to work. To help establish a hove-to balance, what turning tendency should the rudder oppose it with?

A. A tendency to turn the bow towards the wind.

B. A tendency to turn the bow farther away from the wind.

C. No turning tendency, with the rudder centred along the yacht.

**My answer (A–C):** A

### 9. Heaving to, or merely stopping?

With reduced sails and a backed jib, a yacht repeatedly falls off until the breaking seas strike nearly abeam. Its crew proposes leaving the helm because the boat has little forward speed.

Why is low forward speed insufficient evidence that this is a satisfactory hove-to state? What behaviour would you look for while adjusting and testing the balance before relying on this tactic?

**My answer (two or three sentences):** Being hove to also requires a rudder angle that turns the boat a little windward when the backed jib makes it fall  off the wind, then slightly fill to produce forward movement that the rudder will react to. So you should observe the "unstable" equilibrium for a while to assess the balance between these two effects.

### 10. Two drag devices

For this comparison, a stern drogue is designed to restrain a yacht running before the seas, while a bow-deployed parachute sea anchor is designed to hold the bow towards them. Which pairing correctly describes their intended roles?

A. The drogue fixes the yacht’s position over the ground; the sea anchor permits a faster downwind run.

B. The drogue holds the bow towards the seas; the sea anchor restrains stern-first drift.

C. The drogue limits running speed and yaw; the sea anchor resists drift with the bow presented to the seas.

**My answer (A–C):** C

## Integration

### 11. A successful trial, a different location

Offshore, the yacht has previously lain steadily hove to. Today it achieves the same balance, but its measured track over the ground is towards a lee shore at 2 knots. There are 6 nautical miles to the first charted danger on that track. The strong wind is forecast to persist for at least six hours. Assume the track and speed remain constant for this comparison.

Which conclusion follows?

A. The small speed through the water gives at least six hours before reaching the danger.

B. The present track reaches the danger in about three hours, so the balance alone does not make waiting viable.

C. The previous offshore trial establishes that the yacht will stop drifting before it reaches shallow water.

**My answer (A–C):** B

### 12. A faster passage, or a sustainable tactic?

Under reduced sail, the yacht is making useful progress downwind in open water. The experienced helmsman can keep it under control, but must make constant corrections. The only relief helmsman repeatedly allows large yawing excursions, and fatigue is increasing. A stern drogue suitable for the yacht is aboard, with its attachments and deployment arrangements prepared and previously practised.

How does the available crew change your assessment of continuing at the present speed? Explain the benefit you would seek from the drogue and one cost or limitation you would include in the decision.

**My answer (two or three sentences):** The situation is unsustainable safely. So the stern drogue is called for as it will counter the tendency to yaw. One cost is that it will also reduce the speed of the boat. A limitation is that it does not completely prevent the tendency to yaw and so it is not a panacea solution.

## Source and design notes

Section names and allocation come from the repository’s [coverage model](../cunliffe-coverage-model.md). Question design follows the [README](../README.md) and [question-shaping guide](../question-shaping-guide.md).

Supplementary references checked during preparation:

- [Storm tactics — PDF hosted by the Storm Trysail Club](https://stormtrysail.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/storm_tactics.pdf): boat-dependent tactics, wave orientation, steering limits, closures and drag devices.
- [Skip Novak’s Storm Sailing Techniques, Part 4: Heaving to](https://www.yachtingworld.com/video/storm-sailing-techniques-part-4-heaving-to-460): firsthand discussion of balancing a particular yacht, testing its behaviour, leeway and persistent seas after a wind change. His settings are examples for his yacht, not universal instructions.

The questions are original teaching scenarios, not extracts from Cunliffe. Questions 5 and 11 connect sail handling and survival tactics to apparent wind and motion over the ground. Question 12 changes the limiting factor from the yacht’s immediate performance to the crew’s ability to sustain it. Written answers should be assessed on their reasoning; supported alternatives may be defensible where a question asks what to consider.

Further sets could examine trysail arrangements, reefing systems, steering into head seas, preventing accidental gybes, chafe and recovery of deployed gear. This set does not provide deployment instructions or establish practical heavy-weather competence.
