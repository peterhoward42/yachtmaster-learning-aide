# Efficient sailing — starter questions

Based on the recorded headings for chapter 3 of Tom Cunliffe’s *The Complete Yachtmaster*, **10th edition (2021), printed pages 13–22**.

These 15 questions follow the provisional allocation in the [coverage model](../cunliffe-coverage-model.md): 13 questions across the opening material and seven named subsections, plus two integration questions. They use general sailing principles; they have not been verified against the full chapter text or mapped comprehensively to the official RYA syllabus. Section labels identify intended coverage, not confirmed correspondence with a particular passage.

## Instructions

- Assume a conventional cruising monohull sloop with a separate headsail and mainsail, unless stated otherwise.
- Choose **one best answer** for each multiple-choice question. Questions 5 and 15 ask for a brief written answer.
- You can add a short explanation wherever it helps express your thinking.
- Try short sets: **1–5, 6–10, then 11–15**.
- The answer key is deliberately omitted for the initial attempt.

## Opening principles — C03.S01

### 1. More heel, more speed?

On a steady close reach, a yacht heels heavily and needs a large rudder angle to hold course. After reducing sail, she sails more upright and maintains the same speed through the water. Which explanation best fits?

A. Reducing sail has increased the strength of the apparent wind enough to replace all the lost drive.

B. The original sail plan was producing excessive heeling force and steering drag; reducing these can offset the loss of sail area.

C. Rudder drag is unaffected by rudder angle, so the improvement must come entirely from the keel.

D. A displacement yacht always reaches its highest speed with the smallest available sail plan.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Shaping the headsail — C03.S02

### 2. A stalled leeward telltale

You must maintain a steady close-reaching course in open water. The headsail’s windward luff telltale streams aft, but its leeward partner repeatedly hangs limp. The sail is not luffing. Which small adjustment should you try first?

A. Sheet the headsail in further.

B. Move the helm to steer closer to the wind, despite the requirement to hold course.

C. Tighten the mainsail outhaul.

D. Ease the headsail sheet slightly and watch whether both telltales stream.

**My answer (A–D):**

### 3. Reading the sail from top to bottom

In moderate conditions, you slowly head up from a well-trimmed close-hauled course to check the genoa lead position. The upper windward luff telltales lift before the lower ones on repeated checks. You want the sail to begin luffing more evenly along its height. Which change should you trial?

A. Move the sheet lead a little forward to increase the downward pull on the clew and reduce twist.

B. Move the sheet lead a little aft to open the upper leech further.

C. Leave the lead alone and use only the mainsail traveller to alter the genoa’s twist.

D. Move the sheet lead aft because this increases the downward component of sheet tension.

**My answer (A–D):**

### 4. Forestay sag

On a yacht whose rig is designed to control forestay tension with an adjustable backstay, increasing backstay tension reduces forestay sag. Within the rig’s permitted settings, what is the usual effect on the headsail?

A. It makes the headsail fuller and increases its power.

B. It changes only the mainsail; headsail shape is unaffected.

C. It makes the headsail flatter, helping reduce power as the breeze builds.

D. It reduces headsail area in the same way as rolling part of the sail away.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Sail combinations — C03.S04

### 7. Reducing the mainsail alone

Close-hauled, you reef the main but retain a large genoa. The yacht now has a persistent tendency to turn **away from the wind** when the helm is briefly relaxed. Which explanation should you investigate first?

A. Reefing the main must always increase weather helm.

B. The genoa can affect speed but cannot affect balance.

C. The yacht must have developed a steering-system fault at the moment you reefed.

D. The combined sail force may have moved too far forward; the headsail size and the balance of the whole sail plan need reassessment.

**My answer (A–D):**

### 8. One genoa for every wind strength?

You have both a roller-furling genoa and a smaller working jib aboard. Before departure, a sustained fresh upwind passage is forecast. The genoa becomes markedly baggy when deeply furled. Which is the strongest reason to consider the working jib?

A. Its intended working shape may give better drive and control than the deeply furled genoa at a similar exposed area.

B. Any sail with less area necessarily produces more drive.

C. Roller furling preserves the original sail shape regardless of how much sail is rolled away.

D. A smaller headsail removes the need to assess mainsail area or balance.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Hull balance — C03.S05

### 9. Recognising weather helm

In clear water, a yacht persistently tries to turn towards the wind when the helm is briefly relaxed. What is this tendency called, and why does an excessive amount matter?

A. Lee helm; it means the yacht needs more mainsail in every case.

B. Neutral helm; the rudder is producing no drag.

C. Weather helm; holding course with a large opposing rudder angle adds drag and steering load.

D. Leeway; it can be corrected only by changing the compass course.

**My answer (A–D):**

### 10. Sail balance and heel

As the wind strengthens, the yacht heels further and weather helm increases. A crew member proposes changing the relative sizes of the main and headsail while accepting the same heavy heel. What is missing from that diagnosis?

A. Weather helm is determined solely by the relative sail areas.

B. Heel itself changes the hull’s balance and can increase weather helm, so reducing heel also needs consideration.

C. Hull shape matters only when motoring.

D. More rudder angle always improves sailing efficiency, so no adjustment is needed.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Shortening sail — C03.S06

### 11. Choosing when to reef

On an exposed reach, gusts increasingly require large helm corrections and repeated mainsheet easing. Your crew can reef comfortably now; the forecast indicates a further increase in wind. What is the best next decision?

A. Keep full sail until the first uncontrolled round-up proves it is necessary to reef.

B. Keep full sail because easing the sheet in each gust proves the sail plan is suitable for the next hour.

C. Wait until the crew are struggling to handle the reefing gear.

D. Shorten sail while the boat and crew remain manageable, then reassess trim and balance.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Cruising chutes — C03.S08

### 13. A chute hidden behind the main

In light wind and open water, your asymmetric cruising chute repeatedly collapses when you steer almost directly downwind. It fills steadily when you head up onto a broad reach. What is the most likely explanation?

A. The halyard must be too short whenever the yacht points directly downwind.

B. The chute needs a stronger wind because yacht heading cannot affect its airflow.

C. Near dead downwind, the mainsail blankets the chute; heading up brings it into clearer airflow.

D. A cruising chute is intended only for close-hauled sailing.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Integration

### 14. Did the adjustment actually help?

In steady wind, you test a trim change on the same heading. Speed through the water falls slightly, heel increases and holding course requires more rudder. Which conclusion is best supported?

A. Keep the change because more heel is a reliable sign of more useful drive.

B. The observations suggest worse efficiency; reverse the change and compare again before adopting it.

C. Keep the change because additional rudder angle cannot affect performance.

D. Judge solely by how smooth the sail looks, regardless of the yacht’s response.
