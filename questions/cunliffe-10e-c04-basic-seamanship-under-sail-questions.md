# Basic seamanship under sail — starter questions

Based on the recorded headings for chapter 4 of Tom Cunliffe’s *The Complete Yachtmaster*, **10th edition (2021), printed pages 23–34**.

These 18 questions follow the provisional allocation in the [coverage model](../cunliffe-coverage-model.md): 16 questions across ten named subsections, plus two integration questions. They use general sailing principles; they have not been verified against the full chapter text or mapped comprehensively to the official RYA syllabus. Section labels identify intended coverage, not confirmed correspondence with a particular passage.

## Instructions

- Assume a conventional cruising monohull sloop with a separate headsail and mainsail, unless stated otherwise. Reefing questions assume slab reefing except where another system is specified.
- Choose **one best answer** for each multiple-choice question. Questions 9 and 18 ask for a brief written answer.
- You can add a short explanation wherever it helps express your thinking.
- Try short sets: **1–6, 7–12, then 13–18**.
- The answer key is deliberately omitted for the initial attempt.

## Tacking — C04.S01

### 1. Losing way in the turn

In light wind and short chop, your yacht repeatedly stops head to wind during a tack. You have been starting each turn while sailing very slowly, with the sails barely drawing. There is ample clear water. Which change most directly addresses the likely cause?

A. Start from an even slower speed so the bow has less momentum to overcome.

B. Establish useful boat speed before beginning a positive, controlled turn through the wind.

C. Put the helm hard over and hold it there regardless of boat speed or heading.

D. Sheet the new headsail fully home before the bow reaches head to wind.

**My answer (A–D):**

### 2. Releasing the old sheet

During a normal tack, the headsail begins to luff as the bow approaches head to wind. You intend to let it cross freely, without deliberately backing it. Why release the old working sheet at this stage rather than keep it tight until the sail fills on the new side?

A. A tight old sheet increases the yacht’s forward drive throughout the turn.

B. Releasing the old sheet changes which side of the mainsail the wind reaches.

C. The old sheet must be released to make the rudder turn the opposite way.

D. Keeping it tight can hold the headsail aback, opposing completion of the tack and loading the sail and sheet.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Gybing — C04.S02

### 3. Controlling the boom

You are preparing a deliberate gybe in moderate wind and clear water. The mainsail is well out on a broad reach. Which preparation best reduces the risk of the boom sweeping violently across?

A. Brief the crew, clear the boom and sheet’s sweep, and bring the boom towards the centreline under mainsheet control before the stern passes through the wind; then ease it under control on the new side.

B. Leave the mainsheet fully eased and rely on the helm alone to slow the boom.

C. Ask a crew member to stand in the boom’s path and catch it.

D. Release the kicking strap completely and let the boom find its own height during the turn.

**My answer (A–D):**

### 4. An unstable downwind course

In a following sea, the yacht repeatedly yaws either side of dead downwind. There is no preventer rigged, and you have clear water to sail a broad reach. Which change most directly reduces the immediate risk of an accidental gybe?

A. Ease the mainsheet farther so the boom has a longer distance to travel.

B. Keep the same heading and ask the crew to listen for the boom moving.

C. Alter onto a broad reach with a clearer margin from the gybe, and trim for that course.

D. Tighten the headsail halyard while keeping the same course.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Poling out a genoa — C04.S03

### 5. Supporting the pole

You plan to pole out the genoa for a downwind leg. Your pole system is designed to use a topping lift and separate fore and aft guys. What is the main benefit of setting those controls correctly, rather than relying on the loaded genoa sheet to hold the pole in place?

A. They keep the headsail drawing even when the yacht turns head to wind.

B. They support and restrain the pole when sheet load changes, including while the sail is eased or handled.

C. They prevent the yacht from yawing in a following sea.

D. They remove the need to check that the pole and fittings suit the loads.

**My answer (A–D):**

### 6. A course change with the genoa poled out

The genoa is poled out to windward with its sheet passing through the pole end. You now need to tack to reach your destination, but there is time and sea room to prepare. What is the best approach?

A. Tack immediately and allow the sail to drag the pole across the foredeck.

B. Release the pole’s mast fitting while the sail and sheet remain fully loaded.

C. Keep the pole in place and use a faster turn to carry the sail around the forestay.

D. Brief the crew and unload and clear the poled-out arrangement in a controlled way before making the tack.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Painless sail reduction — C04.S04

### 7. Choosing the working conditions

You expect to need a reef before rounding an exposed headland. You currently have sea room and relatively sheltered water; beyond the headland, the sea will be rougher. Which decision best reduces the difficulty of the job?

A. Reef in the sheltered water while the boat and crew are manageable, then reassess the sail plan.

B. Wait until beyond the headland so the sail carries maximum load while being reefed.

C. Wait for the first uncontrolled round-up to confirm the forecast.

D. Send the crew to the mast now but postpone explaining their tasks until the turn at the headland.

**My answer (A–D):**

### 8. A loaded mainsail

You start taking a slab reef while the mainsail remains hard sheeted and drawing strongly. The reefing line becomes difficult to haul. What should you address first?

A. Add a longer winch handle and continue without changing the sail load.

B. Release the halyard completely and let the sail drop wherever it falls.

C. Establish a controlled heading and trim that unload the mainsail, with the boom supported as required by this yacht’s reefing arrangement.

D. Tighten the mainsheet further to hold the sail still against the reefing line.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Reefing — C04.S06

### 10. What carries the reefed sail’s load?

A slab reef has been taken. The sail has reef tack and clew attachments designed to carry the working loads, plus small intermediate reef points for gathering loose cloth. Which statement is correct?

A. Tight intermediate reef points should carry the mainsail’s principal loads.

B. The reef tack and clew arrangements carry the intended working loads; intermediate ties gather loose cloth without being used to haul the sail into its reefed shape.

C. The intermediate reef points replace the need to secure the reef clew.

D. Tightening every intermediate tie can compensate for an unsecured reef tack.

**My answer (A–D):**

### 11. Checking the result

After taking a slab reef, the new clew is held down near the boom but remains too far forward, leaving the reefed foot very baggy. Which aspect of the reefing arrangement should you investigate first?

A. Whether the headsail sheet lead is too far aft.

B. Whether the intermediate reef ties need to carry more load.

C. Whether the yacht’s compass has been affected by the sail reduction.

D. Whether the reef clew line’s lead and tension provide the required aft pull as well as downward pull.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Single-line reefing — C04.S07

### 12. Does one line mean one check?

Your single-line reefing system is designed to draw down both the reef tack and clew. While hauling, the line becomes tight, but the tack is still visibly above its intended position. What is the best conclusion?

A. Line tension alone does not establish that the reef is set; unload as needed and check the halyard allowance, line runs and both reef corners before reloading the sail.

B. A tight line proves both corners are correctly positioned, even when the tack is visibly high.

C. The tack can be left high because single-line systems load only the clew.

D. More winch force is the appropriate first response to any resistance in this system.

**My answer (A–D):**

## In-mast mainsails — C04.S08

### 13. Increasing furling resistance

While partly furling an in-mast mainsail, resistance suddenly rises and the sail begins to bunch at the slot. What is the best immediate response?

A. Release the outhaul entirely and continue winching the furling line.

B. Increase winch power until the bunched cloth passes through the slot.

C. Stop forcing the system, keep the yacht controlled, and investigate the sail’s feed, loading and control settings against the instructions for this installation.

D. Tighten the mainsheet fully and continue furling under greater sail load.

**My answer (A–D):**

## In-boom mainsails — C04.S09

### 14. Why does boom angle matter?

Before using an unfamiliar in-boom furling mainsail, why should you establish the manufacturer’s specified boom angle and halyard-control procedure?

A. Boom angle affects only the sail’s appearance once it is fully hoisted.

B. These settings help the sail feed and roll correctly; unsuitable geometry or tension can cause uneven rolling or a jam.

C. The correct angle is identical for every in-boom system, so the instructions merely confirm a universal setting.

D. Once the boom angle is set, the halyard can always be released without control.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Headsail changes — C04.S10

### 15. When to change a hanked-on headsail

You have a large hanked-on genoa set, with no furling system. A sustained increase in wind is forecast. You have a suitable smaller jib and a crew who can make the change comfortably now. What is the strongest reason to consider changing early?

A. A smaller jib will necessarily make the yacht faster on every point of sail.

B. Changing headsails eliminates any need to consider reefing the main.

C. The large genoa becomes easier to lower and contain as the wind strengthens.

D. Lower sail loads and more manageable deck conditions make it easier to control the sail and reduce the crew’s exposure during the change.

**My answer (A–D):**

### 16. Where will the sail go?

Before lowering a hanked-on headsail at sea, the foredeck crew asks how it will be contained. Why should this be settled before releasing the halyard?

A. A loose sail can blow or wash overboard, creating drag and a difficult recovery; the crew need a workable way to gather and secure it.

B. The hanks ensure that every part of the sail must remain on deck.

C. A sail overboard can always be recovered simply by hauling harder on the halyard.

D. Lowering the sail automatically removes all wind and water loads from it.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Integration

### 17. Heaving to near a lee shore

You want a pause to organise a headsail change. The yacht heaves to reliably, but the nearby shore is downwind and your observed drift would carry you towards it. You have enough room to sail farther offshore before starting the work. Which is the best decision?

A. Heave to immediately because a settled heading guarantees a fixed position.

B. Heave to immediately and judge clearance only by whether the sails look quiet.

C. Establish a larger margin of sea room before pausing, then monitor drift and clearance throughout the change.

D. Heave to immediately and leave the helm unattended until the change is complete.

**My answer (A–D):**

## Source notes for question design

The chapter structure and question allocation come from this repository’s [contents reference](../cunliffe-contents-reference.md) and [coverage model](../cunliffe-coverage-model.md). Equipment principles were cross-checked against [Seldén’s rigging and tuning guide](https://support.seldenmast.com/files/00000000/595-540-E.pdf), with model-specific furling instructions available through [Seldén’s assembly and operation manuals](https://support.seldenmast.com/en/technical_info/manuals_for_assembly.html).
