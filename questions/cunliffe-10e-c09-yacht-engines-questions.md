# Yacht engines — starter questions

Topic-based questions organised by the recorded headings for chapter 9 of Tom Cunliffe’s *The Complete Yachtmaster*, **10th edition (2021), printed pages 88–94**.

These 11 questions follow the [coverage model](../cunliffe-coverage-model.md): three for each of the three subsections, plus two integration questions. The full chapter text was not available, so correspondence with Cunliffe’s specific explanations remains unverified. This is an initial topic sample, not a comprehensive assessment of the chapter or official RYA syllabus.

## Instructions

- Assume a cruising yacht with an inboard diesel, a closed freshwater/coolant circuit cooled through a seawater heat exchanger, and a water-injected exhaust, unless stated otherwise.
- Multiple-choice questions have three options and one best answer. Questions 3, 6, 9 and 11 need a short written answer.
- Use the engine’s own manual for component locations, limits and procedures. No dismantling of high-pressure injection equipment is required in these questions.
- In fault scenarios, assume the yacht is secured or otherwise clear of immediate navigational danger. Concentrate on the engine diagnosis and the stated evidence.
- Try two short sessions: **1–6, then 7–11**. The answer key is omitted for the initial attempt. No confidence ratings are needed.

## Basic maintenance — C09.S01

### 1. Two water circuits

Before a cold start, the coolant level is correct, but the seawater inlet seacock is closed. Why does the coolant check alone fail to establish that the engine can keep cool?

A. The coolant removes combustion heat only while the starter motor is turning.

B. The coolant carries heat to the exchanger, but seawater must carry it away.

C. The coolant supplies the exhaust, but seawater lubricates the engine bearings.

**My answer (A–C):** B

### 2. A full sump, but an alarm

The oil level was correct before starting. Once the engine is running, the low-oil-pressure alarm remains on. Which interpretation best explains why the dipstick reading does not settle the matter?

A. The dipstick measures gearbox oil; the alarm measures engine oil.

B. The dipstick measures oil temperature; the alarm measures its quantity.

C. The dipstick measures oil quantity; the alarm concerns its circulation pressure.

**My answer (A–C):** C

### 3. The check after starting

The seawater inlet is open and the strainer looked clear before starting. The engine starts normally, but the usual water discharge from the exhaust is absent.

What would you do with the engine now, and why would waiting for the temperature alarm be a poor way to confirm the problem? Give one possible fault that the pre-start observations have not excluded.

**My answer (two or three sentences):** Turn it off pending investigating the problem. It is bad for the health of the engine to reach the high temperature necessary to trigger the alarm. The pre start checks did not include coolant fluid a level (volume) check.

## Trouble shooting — C09.S02

### 4. Does it turn over?

On pressing the starter, you hear rapid clicking, the panel lights dim markedly, and the engine does not rotate. Which area would you investigate first?

A. Battery charge and the high-current connections to the starter.

B. Fuel supply from the tank through the filters to the injection pump.

C. Seawater flow from the inlet through the pump to the heat exchanger.

**My answer (A–C):** A

### 5. Water outside, heat inside

During motoring, the coolant temperature rises above its normal range. You stop the engine. Before stopping, the exhaust water discharge appeared normal. Which conclusion is justified by that observation?

A. It excludes a cooling fault, so the temperature sender must be faulty.

B. It indicates seawater flow, but leaves coolant circulation and heat transfer in question.

C. It establishes coolant circulation, but leaves seawater flow in question.

**My answer (A–C):** B

### 6. A changed symptom

Compare two failures: in the first, the starter cannot turn the engine; in the second, it turns the engine at its normal starting speed, but the engine does not fire.

Why should these lead you down different initial diagnostic paths? Name the system you would investigate first in each case, without giving a repair procedure.

**My answer (two or three sentences):** If the starter cannot turn the engine, that IS the problem. I can arise from their being insuffient charge in the battery, faulty wiring or connections on the high current circuit, failed starter motor or solenoid/relay, or god forbid a siezed engine. If it turns at starting speed, the problem will be either air supply, fuel supply or injection, or a catastrophic loss of compression.

## Fuel problems — C09.S03

### 7. It idles, but will not work

A diesel runs smoothly at idle but loses speed when asked for sustained power. A fuel-filter restriction indicator shows unusually high suction on the engine side of the primary filter. Which explanation best connects the observations?

A. The propeller is too small, so the engine needs less fuel under load.

B. The starter circuit is weak, so combustion slows once a gear is engaged.

C. The filter passes enough fuel for idle, but restricts the greater flow needed under load.

**My answer (A–C):** C

### 8. A new filter, a new problem

The engine ran normally before its low-pressure fuel filter was changed. It now cranks at normal speed but will not start. There is fuel in the tank and the fuel shut-off is open. Which maintenance-related cause is the strongest initial lead?

A. Air introduced into the fuel supply during the filter change.

B. A discharged starter battery caused by the new filter element.

C. A blocked seawater inlet caused by opening the filter housing.

**My answer (A–C):** A

### 9. The replacement blocks too

After a rough passage, a fuel filter is found loaded with dark debris. A replacement restores power briefly, then blocks in the same way.

What does the recurrence suggest about the source of the problem? Why is repeatedly fitting new filters unlikely to provide a lasting cure? Do these observations alone establish that the debris is microbial contamination?

**My answer (two or three sentences):** The likely cause is "diesel bug". Replacement filters will inevitably clog again. A targeted test is required to confirm the cause - using a propriety testing method.

## Integration

### 10. Same alarm, different timing

An engine has just stopped after fuel starvation. With the panel still switched on, the oil-pressure alarm sounds. Before the stop, oil pressure was normal. Compare this with question 2, where the alarm persists while the engine is running. Which interpretation best fits this new sequence?

A. The alarm proves that loss of oil pressure caused the fuel starvation.

B. The alarm may follow normally from the stopped engine’s oil pump no longer turning.

C. The alarm shows that the fuel filter and oil filter share a pressure circuit.

**My answer (A–C):** B

### 11. Why repeated starting attempts can add a second fault

A yacht has a waterlift exhaust: cooling seawater collects in a chamber and is expelled by exhaust gases. After running out of diesel, the engine cranks but does not fire. Its seawater pump still turns during cranking.

Apart from battery depletion and starter heating, what additional risk can repeated unsuccessful cranking create? Explain the water-and-gas imbalance, and identify which installation-specific instructions you would consult before further attempts.

**My answer (two or three sentences):** I can infer the answer to this one partially, but please give me a fuller answer also. The water collection chamber is supposed to buffer water waiting to be expelled by significant exhaust gas flow. It wil be charged during all engine cranking not only when the engine is firing. So it can become full during unsuccessful cranking, where there is no combustion-amplified exhaust flow - potentially causing too much back pressure in the exaust or permitting water to drain back to the engine. I would guess that the manual would offer a manual draining method.

## Source and design notes

Section names and allocation come from the repository’s [coverage model](../cunliffe-coverage-model.md). Question design follows the [README](../README.md) and [question-shaping guide](../question-shaping-guide.md).

Supplementary technical references are the RYA’s [Engine checks & preventing fuel contamination](https://www.rya.org.uk/water-safety/boat-safety-and-maintenance/engine-checks-preventing-fuel-contamination/) for pre-start and exhaust-discharge checks; [Dealing with diesel engines](https://www.rya.org.uk/water-safety/boat-safety-and-maintenance/dealing-with-diesel-engines/) for maintenance and contamination checks; and the [YANMAR YM Series Operation Manual](https://www.yanmar.com/marine/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/0AYMM-M0002E_2025.02.pdf) for cooling-system distinctions, starting faults, fuel-system air and waterlift-exhaust cranking precautions. The YANMAR manual illustrates one engine family; it does not establish the procedure for every yacht engine or verify Cunliffe’s treatment.

The set samples causal understanding and diagnostic reasoning rather than detailed repair skills. Questions 2 and 10 change the alarm’s timing to test interpretation; questions 4 and 6 distinguish failure to crank from failure to fire. Further sets could extend coverage to belts, impellers, tank ventilation, water separation and charging faults. Answering these questions does not demonstrate practical maintenance competence.
