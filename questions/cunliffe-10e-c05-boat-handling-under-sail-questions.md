# Boat handling under sail — starter questions

Based on the recorded headings for chapter 5 of Tom Cunliffe’s *The Complete Yachtmaster*, **10th edition (2021), printed pages 35–43**.

These 14 questions follow the provisional allocation in the [coverage model](../cunliffe-coverage-model.md): 12 questions across seven named subsections, plus two integration questions. They use general sailing principles; they have not been verified against the full chapter text or mapped comprehensively to the official RYA syllabus. Section labels identify intended coverage, not confirmed correspondence with a particular passage.

## Instructions

- Assume a conventional cruising monohull sloop with a separate headsail and mainsail, unless stated otherwise. Manoeuvres are under sail, without engine thrust.
- Choose **one best answer** for each multiple-choice question. Questions 4 and 14 ask for a brief written answer.
- You can add a short explanation wherever it helps express your thinking.
- Try short sets: **1–5, 6–10, then 11–14**.
- The answer key is deliberately omitted for the initial attempt.

## No brakes — C05.S01

### 1. Easing the sheets too late

In sheltered water with negligible tide, you approach a mooring under mainsail alone on a close reach. You ease the sheet until the sail flaps, but the yacht continues towards the buoy faster than intended. What best explains this?

A. A flapping sail must still be producing the same forward drive as a drawing sail.

B. Removing most sail drive does not instantly remove the yacht’s momentum; slowing needs distance and time.

C. Easing the mainsheet directly increases the yacht’s momentum.

D. A yacht cannot slow unless its bow points exactly into the wind.

**My answer (A–D):** B

### 2. Learning the stopping distance

You are handling an unfamiliar yacht and have ample clear water near the intended mooring. Which preparation would most usefully improve your judgement of the final approach?

A. Adopt a stopping distance based only on the yacht’s length.

B. Assume she will lose speed exactly as your previous yacht did.

C. Make the first speed-control trial within a boat length of the buoy.

D. Rehearse the intended approach heading and sail plan in clear water, observing how she slows, accelerates and steers.

**My answer (A–D):** D

## Wind awareness — C05.S02

### 3. A sheltered final approach

A steady breeze is driving you towards a mooring, but the final part of the approach lies behind a high breakwater. You expect the wind there to become much lighter and less steady. Why does this matter to your plan?

A. The shelter may remove the drive needed for the last part of the approach or escape, so you should allow for the change before entering it.

B. Shelter changes the wind’s strength but can never change its direction.

C. Boat speed and steerage will remain unchanged when the sail loses its drive.

D. The masthead wind indication outside the shelter establishes the wind throughout the approach.

**My answer (A–D):** A

## Tide awareness — C05.S03

### 4. Stationary, but still steering

In a steady tidal stream of two knots, your yacht points directly into the stream and sails through the water at two knots. She remains alongside a fixed mooring buoy without touching it.

Explain why the rudder can still provide steering control although the yacht makes no progress over the ground. Which speed matters most when judging how quickly you are closing a fixed buoy?

**My answer (two or three sentences):** The boat is still sailing in this situation. The boat is moving through water and there is water passing over the rudder. The position relative to the buoy is unchanging because the sailing way of the boat is compensated by the the body of water she is sailing moving the opposite way at the same rate. The speed that matters and provides governance is the speed at which the boat is closing the buoy.

## Sailing slowly — C05.S04

### 5. Controlling the last part of the approach

In a steady moderate breeze with negligible tide, you approach a mooring under mainsail alone on a close reach. Trials have shown that easing the sheet makes the sail flap and sheeting in restores drive. You are still well clear of the buoy. How should you regulate speed?

A. Hold the mainsheet fully in and use large rudder angles as the only speed control.

B. Turn dead downwind so the mainsail can always be completely depowered by easing its sheet.

C. Ease or trim the sheet progressively, watching the yacht’s response and retaining enough way to steer.

D. Let the yacht lose all steerage immediately, then rely on the rudder to bring her to the buoy.

**My answer (A–D):** C

### 6. Too slow to turn

In clear water with negligible tide, you slow almost to a stop under a flapping mainsail. A large helm movement produces very little change of heading. The rudder is clear and undamaged. What is the most likely cause?

A. The rudder needs the sail to be fully sheeted in before it can turn at any speed.

B. There is too little water flow over the rudder for it to generate much turning force.

C. The rudder acts on speed over the ground rather than movement through the water.

D. A large helm angle guarantees the same turning force regardless of water speed.

**My answer (A–D):** B

## Mooring under sail — C05.S05

### 7. Wind against tide

The tidal stream is strong enough that you plan to approach a buoy heading into it. This heading puts the wind nearly astern. A crew member proposes keeping the mainsail set and relying on easing its sheet to stop the drive. Which response best addresses the problem?

A. Easing the main always removes its drive, whatever the wind angle.

B. Approaching into the tide guarantees that the yacht cannot overrun the buoy.

C. Keep both sails fully drawing because the tide will provide all necessary speed control.

D. Test a suitable reduced sail plan and approach in clear water first; a following wind may keep the main driving even with its sheet eased, so lowering it and using a controllable headsail may be more suitable. 

**My answer (A–D):** D

### 8. Briefing the bow crew

Before the final approach, which briefing gives the crew the most useful information for picking up the mooring?

A. Allocate the pickup and securing tasks, agree clear distance and speed calls, and explain when to abandon the pickup and try again.

B. Ask the bow crew to concentrate on catching the buoy and leave all communication until after contact.

C. Tell the crew to hold the yacht’s full weight by hand if the pickup line comes tight.

D. Leave the securing line unprepared until the buoy is within reach, to avoid distracting the crew.

**My answer (A–D):** A

### 9. Missing the pickup

The bow crew misses the pickup buoy. You still have steerage and a clear, preplanned route away, but continuing would carry the yacht over the mooring tackle. What is the best action?

A. Ask a crew member to jump into the water with a line.

B. Continue over the buoy so the crew can reach it from the stern.

C. Abandon the pickup, keep clear of the tackle and sail away on the escape route before setting up another attempt.

D. Ask the crew to use the boat hook to arrest the yacht’s movement against the buoy.

**My answer (A–D):** C

## Anchoring — C05.S06

### 10. Ready before arrival

You intend to anchor under sail in a sheltered, uncrowded anchorage. Depth, holding ground and swinging room are suitable, and wind and tide permit a controlled approach. What should be settled before committing to the final approach?

A. Only the position where the anchor will be dropped; the crew can work out cable handling afterwards.

B. The sail and speed-control plan, anchor and cable readiness, crew roles, and a route away if the approach fails.

C. Only the amount of mainsheet to ease; the anchor will compensate for any remaining speed.

D. A plan to release the anchor at cruising speed so it can stop the yacht immediately.

**My answer (A–D):** B

### 11. Has the anchor held?

After lowering the anchor under sail and paying out suitable cable, the yacht settles back and the cable takes load. What gives the best evidence that the anchor is holding?

A. The anchor is heavy enough for the yacht according to its label.

B. The cable has become taut once, even though the yacht continues moving towards the shore.

C. The sails are down and the deck is quiet.

D. Repeated position checks against fixed references show the yacht remaining within an expected swinging area as the anchor takes load.

**My answer (A–D):** D

## Berthing — C05.S07

### 12. An unsuitable berth

Your engine has failed. You are safely outside a marina with room to sail or wait. The allocated berth lies at the end of a narrow, crowded fairway, where the wind would drive you towards the closed end and there is no reliable escape under sail. Harbour staff can arrange assistance. What is the best decision?

A. Remain in a safe position and arrange assistance or a more suitable destination before entering.

B. Enter at higher speed to preserve steerage until the last moment.

C. Enter because a yacht that can reach the fairway entrance must also be able to stop in its berth.

D. Rely on the crew pushing off neighbouring boats to control the final approach.

**My answer (A–D):** A

## Integration

### 13. The approach changes

You have rehearsed a mainsail-only mooring approach in open water. Before you commit, the wind shifts so that on the intended final heading the mainsail now remains drawing even with the sheet fully eased. You have room to sail clear. What should you do?

A. Keep the original approach because the rehearsal established a permanent stopping distance.

B. Approach faster so momentum carries the yacht through any uncertainty.

C. Sail clear and reassess the approach and sail plan, testing that you can control drive under the new conditions before trying again.

D. Continue and ask the pickup crew to stop the yacht with the mooring line.

**My answer (A–D):** C

### 14. Turning a plan into a controlled manoeuvre

In moderate wind, you plan to pick up a mooring under sail in an unfamiliar tidal harbour. There is clear water nearby for a trial approach, and you have not yet committed to the mooring.

Name three things you would establish before committing: one about wind and tide, one about the yacht’s speed control, and one about what you will do if the pickup fails. Explain briefly how each affects your plan. Exact courses and line-handling sequences are not required.

**My answer (three short points):** Work out if the mooring dynamics are best characterised as wind with tide, or vice versa or that tide is neglible. Having decided, based on those priors decide which approach direction is best, and thence which sail plan. Then try our the effectiveness of sheeting to control speed up or down thus. Also plan an exit route if the pickup fails consistent with the known constraints. 

## Source notes for question design

The chapter structure and question allocation come from this repository’s [contents reference](../cunliffe-contents-reference.md) and [coverage model](../cunliffe-coverage-model.md). Approach planning and sail choice were cross-checked against sailing school [Sailtrain’s guide to picking up a mooring buoy under sail](https://sailtrain.org.uk/how-to-pick-mooring-buoy-sail/). Observation of wind and current, low approach speed and mooring preparation were also checked against [Maritime Safety Victoria’s mooring guidance](https://handbook.safetransport.vic.gov.au/boating-rules/basic-boat-handling-tips/mooring/). These supplementary sources do not establish Cunliffe’s exact treatment. The questions require the approach to be assessed for the particular yacht and conditions rather than prescribing one universal manoeuvre.
