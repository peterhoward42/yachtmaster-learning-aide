# Ropes and ropework — starter questions

Topic-based questions organised by the recorded headings for chapter 7 of Tom Cunliffe’s *The Complete Yachtmaster*, **10th edition (2021), printed pages 60–72**.

These 20 questions follow the [coverage model](../cunliffe-coverage-model.md): 18 across nine subsections, plus two integration questions. The full chapter text was not available, so correspondence with Cunliffe’s specific explanations remains unverified. This is an initial topic sample, not a comprehensive assessment of the chapter or official RYA syllabus.

## Instructions

- Multiple-choice questions have three options and one best answer. Questions 2, 4, 7, 9, 11, 17, 19 and 20 ask for a brief written answer.
- Assume a cruising monohull. Lines, fittings and attachment points are suitable for the stated task unless a question asks you to assess them. Mooring scenarios use a floating pontoon unless stated otherwise.
- Interpret line geometry from the stated attachment points rather than relying on names such as “forward spring”.
- Try four short sessions: **1–5, 6–10, 11–15, then 16–20**. No confidence ratings are needed. The answer key is omitted for the initial attempt.
- These questions test understanding. Tying, inspecting and handling ropes still need practical demonstration.

## Types of rope — C07.S01

### 1. Stretch for which job?

Two ropes have adequate strength and fit the relevant fittings. One is relatively elastic nylon; the other is a low-stretch halyard rope. Which pairing best matches their properties to the task?

A. Nylon for a halyard; low-stretch rope for a mooring warp exposed to surge.

B. Nylon for a mooring warp exposed to surge; low-stretch rope for a halyard.

C. Nylon for both jobs, because extension reduces changes in sail shape and mooring loads.

**My answer (A–C):** B

### 2. What changes at the sail?

A halyard stretches appreciably as the wind strengthens, although it has not slipped in its clutch. What happens to luff tension, and how might that affect the sail’s shape? Give the causal connection in one or two sentences.

**My answer:** The stretched halyard conveys less tensioning force to the luff. That causes the sail to adopt a fuller shape, with the point of maximum camber further aft, which reduces pointing ability and performance when close hauled.

## Wire halyards — C07.S02

### 3. A replacement that fits the groove?

You plan to replace a wire-to-rope halyard with an all-rope halyard. The existing masthead sheave has a narrow groove shaped for wire. What determines whether you can retain that sheave?

A. Whether the new rope has at least the wire’s breaking strength.

B. Whether the new rope’s diameter matches the old rope tail.

C. Whether its groove, diameter and condition suit the new rope.

**My answer (A–C):** C

### 4. What does a “meat hook” tell you?

An inspection finds small broken wire ends sticking out of a wire halyard near its terminal. Besides the risk to hands, what does this indicate about the halyard? Would covering that area with tape establish that it is fit for service? Explain briefly.

**My answer:** Halyard is seriously deterioated and should be replaced.

## Knots, splices and whippings — C07.S03

### 5. A loop or a stopper?

You need a temporary fixed loop in the end of an ordinary polyester rope to pass over a bollard. It will carry a steady mooring load and must later be untied. Which knot is intended for this job?

A. A bowline.

B. A figure-of-eight stopper.

C. A reef knot.

**My answer (A–C):** A

### 6. Joining unequal ropes

You need to join two ordinary, non-slippery ropes of noticeably different diameters for a light, non-critical task. Which arrangement is appropriate?

A. A reef knot, with the thicker rope forming both crossing turns.

B. A double sheet bend, with the thicker rope forming the bight.

C. A bowline in the thinner rope, with the thicker rope passed loose through its eye.

**My answer (A–C):** B

### 7. Does the label still describe the assembly?

A new rope’s published breaking strength is comfortably above a proposed load. Someone adds a knot and says the assembly still has the rope’s published strength. What is wrong with that inference? Explain why a correctly made, manufacturer-specified splice is often preferred for a permanent eye, without quoting a universal percentage.

**My answer (two short points):** A knot significantly lower's a ropes strength. A splice also lowers the strength, but less so.

## Eyes and ends — C07.S04

### 8. Which part does a whipping protect?

The end of a three-strand rope is beginning to unlay. What is the principal purpose of applying a sound whipping near the end?

A. To restore the strength of any damaged load-bearing strands.

B. To increase friction where the rope passes around a cleat.

C. To bind the strands together and prevent further unlaying.

**My answer (A–C):** C

### 9. Two permanent eyes, two different jobs

One eye will repeatedly bear against a metal shackle; another must slip over a large pontoon bollard. What is a thimble intended to do in the first eye, and why might a small thimbled eye be unsuitable for the second job?

**My answer (one or two sentences):** A thimble in the first eye will mitigate wear of the rope from chafing in the first case by creating metal to metal to metal contact. In the second case the loop must be much larger than the bollard so as to slip over easily, and light and flexible to facilitate the manual handling required.

## Rope handling — C07.S05

### 10. The tail becomes hard to hold

A sheet is correctly led around a winch, with turns lying neatly side by side. It is not in the self-tailer. Under a steady load the crew find that the tail needs excessive hand force. Which change most directly reduces the required tailing force, within the winch’s instructions?

A. Remove a turn so less rope touches the drum.

B. Add a turn so more rope touches the drum.

C. Pull the tail vertically so the turns overlap.

**My answer (A–C):** B

### 11. A coil that will not run

A braided line repeatedly kinks when paid out from a tight circular coil. You need it to run freely for the next manoeuvre. How could you lay it out differently, and what should you do about twist already present in it?

**My answer (one or two sentences):** Uncoil it completely, then flake it.

## Securing the yacht — C07.S06

### 12. Which line arrests ahead movement?

A yacht lies parallel to a pontoon. Which line becomes taut and opposes the yacht moving **ahead** along the berth?

A. A line from near the bow to a pontoon cleat well aft of that point.

B. A line from near the stern to a pontoon cleat well forward of that point.

C. A line from amidships at right angles to the yacht to a pontoon cleat.

**My answer (A–C):** A

### 13. The pontoon no longer floats

You move from a floating pontoon to a fixed quay with a substantial tidal range. Assume adequate depth and suitable fendering. Why can short, tight breast ropes become a problem as the water level changes?

A. They become springs and allow excessive fore-and-aft travel.

B. They lose their ability to carry tension when their angle steepens.

C. They may prevent the yacht following the water level freely.

**My answer (A–C):** C

## Coming alongside — C07.S07

### 14. Where should the first line lead?

The yacht is alongside a long floating pontoon, still moving slowly ahead. The immediate aim is to check that movement using a line from an amidships cleat. Which shore cleat gives the required lead?

A. A cleat well ahead of the yacht’s amidships cleat.

B. A cleat well aft of the yacht’s amidships cleat.

C. A cleat directly abeam of the yacht’s amidships cleat.

**My answer (A–C):** B

### 15. Recovering a slipped line

A departure line runs from an onboard cleat, around a smooth shore bollard and back aboard. Both ends are accessible aboard. Which arrangement allows the line to be slipped and recovered without sending someone ashore?

A. Both ends remain made fast aboard while the yacht motors away.

B. The shore bight is tightened into a fixed eye around the bollard.

C. One end is released and runs freely around the bollard as the other is recovered.

**My answer (A–C):** C

## Towing — C07.S08

### 16. Sharing the tow load

A yacht being towed has two suitable strong points near the bow. A symmetric bridle leads from them to the towline. Compare a narrow V with a very wide V, carrying the same steady forward tow force. What happens to tension in each bridle leg as the V becomes very wide?

A. It increases because less of each leg’s pull acts forwards.

B. It decreases because the attachment points are farther apart.

C. It stays the same because each leg carries half the tow force.

**My answer (A–C):** A

### 17. The towline snatches

In a swell, a towline repeatedly goes slack and then comes taut with a sharp jerk. Why can the peak load greatly exceed the steady towing load? Give two changes to the towing arrangement or operation that could reduce snatching, explaining the mechanism of each. Do not assume that simply fitting a stronger line solves the cause.

**My answer (three short points):** Both boats heave due to wave motion independently which shortens and lengthens
the tow line. These movements give each boat significant kinetic energy which must be countered by forces from the tow line. One remediation is to hang a weight from the middle of the tow line to absorb some of it by rising and falling. Towing alongside will reduce snatching.

## Towing with the dinghy — C07.S09

### 18. Control in a confined harbour

In sheltered, calm water, a suitable powered dinghy is lashed securely alongside a yacht with an engine failure. Both crews remain aboard their own craft. Compared with a long tow astern, what specific handling advantage can this arrangement provide at very low speed?

A. It gives the yacht’s rudder normal steering force even when stopped.

B. It lets the dinghy transmit thrust and turning force directly through the lashings.

C. It makes both craft pivot around the yacht’s bow whatever the dinghy’s position.

**My answer (A–C):** B

## Integration

### 19. The line is holding, but the bow is not

In calm water, a yacht’s first line runs from amidships to a pontoon cleat aft. It has checked the last of her ahead movement. The bow is still slightly angled away from the pontoon. Why does making that line fast not, by itself, establish that the bow is secured alongside? Identify the movement still available.

**My answer (one or two sentences):** I am assuming that by "that line" you mean the checking line. That line alone offers the boat liberty to move aft which would slacken that line and consequently allow the boat to drift away from the pontoon, in particular with a rotation that moved the bow away from the pontoon.

### 20. Change the rope; change the weak point

A yacht surges at her berth. Her elastic mooring warp stretches and recovers without damage, but you want less movement. You replace it with a much lower-stretch rope of adequate breaking strength, keeping the same lead and length.

What trade-off have you introduced between movement and peak load? If the new rope then chafes at a rough fairlead, why is fitting an even stronger rope an incomplete remedy? Give one change aimed at the chafe itself.

**My answer (two or three sentences):** The trade off is less movement in return for higher peak loads. You need to protect the line from chafe - typically with a tubular sleeve.
