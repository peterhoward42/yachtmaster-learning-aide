# Charts, publications and chart table tools — starter questions

Topic-based questions organised by the recorded headings for chapter 12 of Tom Cunliffe’s *The Complete Yachtmaster*, **10th edition (2021), printed pages 107–118**.

These 18 questions follow the [coverage model](../cunliffe-coverage-model.md): 16 section questions and two integration questions. The chapter text was not available, so correspondence with Cunliffe’s specific treatment remains unverified. This is a first sample of the listed topics, not comprehensive chapter or RYA syllabus coverage.

## Instructions

- Multiple-choice questions have three options and one best answer. Other questions need a short written answer; a sentence or two normally suffices.
- All scenarios and numbers are training examples. No actual chart is required.
- Assume conventional north-up Mercator paper charts where specified. Distinguish true and magnetic direction only where asked.
- Try three short sessions: **1–6, 7–12, then 13–18**. The answer key is omitted for the initial attempt. No confidence ratings are needed.

## Latitude, longitude and the globe — C12.S01

### 1. Crossing Greenwich

A yacht moves from **50°20.0′N, 000°10.0′W** to **50°20.0′N, 000°05.0′E**. Which describes its change of position?

A. Five minutes of longitude west, with no change in latitude.

B. Fifteen minutes of longitude east, with no change in latitude.

C. Fifteen minutes of latitude north, with no change in longitude.

**My answer (A–C):** B

### 2. Equal longitude differences

Two short east–west legs each span ten minutes of longitude. One is near the equator; the other is at 60°N. Which is shorter on the Earth’s surface, and what feature of the globe explains the difference?

**My answer:** The more Northern is shorter. Because lines of longitude are more progressively more closely spaced as they approach the poles. Converging to a point at the poles.

## Chart projections — C12.S02

### 3. A straight line on Mercator

Away from the poles, what does a straight line on a Mercator chart represent?

A. A route with a constant true bearing, which need not be the shortest route over the globe.

B. The shortest route over the globe, whose true bearing generally changes along it.

C. A route with a constant magnetic bearing, regardless of local variation.

**My answer (A–C):** A

### 4. Measuring a distance

You take a short coastal distance between dividers on a Mercator chart. Which scale should you use to express it in nautical miles?

A. The longitude scale nearest the middle of the measured line.

B. Any part of the latitude scale, because its spacing is constant over the sheet.

C. The latitude scale near the latitude of the measured line, using one minute as approximately one nautical mile.

**My answer (A–C):** B

## Direction — C12.S03

### 5. Two rings on a compass rose

A chart’s compass rose has an outer ring referenced to true north and an inner ring referenced to magnetic north. You measure a course using the outer ring, then label the result “magnetic”.

What reference error have you made? Why does transferring the same number to the steering compass not resolve it? You do not need to calculate a correction or discuss deviation.

**My answer:** Magnetic north deviates from true north in the form of a continuous gradual drift. Your chart measured course is relative to true north. The steering compass shows direction relative to magnetic north. This mis labelling misleads the helmsman to believe that the adjustment is already present in the bearing given to him.

## Scale and passage requirements — C12.S04

### 6. The harbour entrance

You have current charts at **1:200,000** and **1:20,000**, both covering a harbour entrance. Which statement correctly compares their use for examining that entrance?

A. The 1:200,000 chart is larger scale and normally gives more entrance detail.

B. The 1:20,000 chart is larger scale and normally gives more entrance detail.

C. The scales give equivalent entrance detail if both charts are printed on the same paper size.

**My answer (A–C):** B

## Chart symbols — C12.S05

### 7. Rock, wreck or something else?

You cannot confidently identify a small chart symbol beside a planned track. Where would you look up its precise meaning for that chart series, and why would interpreting only its colour and resemblance to a familiar symbol be insufficient?

**My answer:** Look it up in the almanac. A properly looked up identification of the symbol carries no ambiguity. The resemblance method does.

### 8. An underlined height

On a metric paper chart, a figure is identified by the chart’s symbol convention as a **drying height of 1.2 metres**. What does it describe?

A. A seabed feature standing 1.2 metres above chart datum.

B. A seabed feature lying 1.2 metres below chart datum.

C. A seabed feature standing 1.2 metres above the current sea surface.

**My answer (A–C):** A

## Other information on the chart — C12.S06

### 9. More digits than the survey supports

A plotter displays your position to several decimal places. The chart’s source information shows that the surrounding seabed was surveyed long ago using widely spaced sounding lines.

Does the detailed position display establish that a route between the charted soundings is free of uncharted obstructions? Explain which uncertainty remains even if the yacht’s position is accurate.

**My answer:** The soundings based on old methods and widely spaced samples contain a greater risk of shallower water in between the soundings.

## Chart datums — C12.S07

### 10. Two kinds of reference

A chart note discusses the adjustment needed to plot satellite-derived positions. Another note defines the reference level for soundings. Which distinction matters?

A. Both notes concern the reference level for depth, so a tidal-height adjustment resolves both.

B. Both notes concern latitude and longitude, so a position adjustment resolves both.

C. One concerns horizontal position and the other vertical levels; resolving one does not resolve the other.

**My answer (A–C):** C

## Yachting charts — C12.S08

### 11. A convenient sheet

A yacht-sized chart pack includes a harbour plan with enlarged lettering, marina annotations and a convenient fold. You want to know whether its underlying hydrographic information is more recent than that in another chart of the same harbour.

Name two checks that would help establish this. Explain why the leisure-oriented presentation alone does not answer the question.

**My answer:** Please educate me on the answer to this one.

## Electronic charts — C12.S09

### 12. Zooming into a raster chart

Your plotter displays a raster chart: a digital image of a paper chart. You zoom in repeatedly, without loading a different chart. What changes?

A. The underlying survey becomes more detailed as the displayed scale increases.

B. The existing chart image becomes larger without adding underlying survey detail.

C. Previously uncharted shoals are added as the existing image becomes larger.

**My answer (A–C):** B

## Chart corrections — C12.S10

### 13. Bought yesterday

You buy a new paper chart yesterday. Its correction record stops several months before the purchase date. A recent relevant Notice to Mariners relocates a buoy shown near your planned track.

Which date or record establishes the chart’s update state? What should happen to the buoy information before you use the chart for that passage?

**My answer:** Assuming you are aware of the Notice to Mariners, you should manually annotate your paper chart with the changed buoy position.

## The Nautical Almanac — C12.S11

### 14. Which sort of almanac?

You need harbour contact details and local tidal information for a coastal passage. One book is a cruising almanac containing port information and tide tables. The other is an astronomical Nautical Almanac containing celestial-body data for sight reduction.

Which is the relevant starting point for those particular needs, and why does the shared word “almanac” not make the books interchangeable?

**My answer:** I have browsed in some detail an old "classic" / "traditional" almanac, but I don't know which of your categories that is. The one i browsed was immense and had very wide ranging advice including topics such as first aid and engine maintenance as well as the more obvious harbour charts. So I don't know enough to answer this. I know that celestial-body derived tidal prediction is insufficient for the local-knowledge tideal stream patterns in a harboar approach.

## Pilot books and sailing directions — C12.S12

### 15. Depth is not the whole approach

Your chart shows sufficient depth in a harbour entrance. You also want to know whether an onshore swell makes that entrance difficult, and which local landmarks help identify it.

Which source is most directly suited to this additional information?

A. A chart-symbol reference explaining the symbols already printed on the chart.

B. An astronomical almanac giving the positions of celestial bodies.

C. An up-to-date pilot book or sailing directions describing the approach.

**My answer (A–C):** C

## Chart table tools — C12.S13

### 16. Carrying a direction across the chart

You have drawn a route leg on a paper chart. Using parallel rulers, you transfer its direction to the compass rose. During the transfer, one ruler slips and the pair rotates slightly.

Why is the resulting bearing unreliable even if you read the compass rose precisely? State the geometric property that a correct transfer must preserve.

**My answer:** Parallelism (obviously).

## Integration

### 17. The depth calculation and its limit

A training chart shows a sounding of **2.4 metres below chart datum**. The predicted tide height at the relevant time is **1.6 metres above the same datum**. The yacht’s draught is **1.8 metres**.

Ignoring waves, atmospheric effects, squat and prediction or survey errors, calculate the water depth and under-keel clearance at the sounding. Then explain why those figures alone do not establish the clearance along the entire approach.

**My answer:** At lowest astronomical tide conditions the depth of water is 2.4m. The predicted state of tide for the approach adds 1.6 to that, giving 4m.  The yacht's draught "uses up" 1.8m. So the expected under keel clearance becomes 2.2m. The sounding is a single point data. The depth contours shown by the chart around that point are also required to show how depth changes along your source towards that point.

### 18. Three views, one source

A paper chart, a raster image of it on a tablet, and a harbour diagram in a pilot book all show a buoy in the same position. You discover that all three used the same older chart information. A current local harbour notice reports that the buoy has been moved.

Why does agreement between the three older views provide less reassurance than it first appears? Which information needs reconciling before you use the buoy to identify the approach?

**My answer:** The paper chart and the raster image are the SAME data. The information in the pilot is stated to be from those superceded dated sources. The local harbour notice wins.
